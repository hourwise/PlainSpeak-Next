# Choosing a bicycle lock

Locks are rated by how long they resist an attack, not by whether they resist
one. Every lock on the market can be defeated given enough time and the right
tool. What you are buying is minutes.

The ratings that matter are Sold Secure and ART. Both test against a defined
set of tools for a defined period. A Sold Secure Diamond lock has survived five
minutes of sustained attack by a trained tester. Gold is three minutes. Silver
is one. Specifically, the tester is given a fixed tool set and a clock.

In addition, insurers set their own requirements. Many policies name a rating
directly, and a claim can be refused if the lock used was below it. Check the
policy before you buy the lock rather than after. Similarly, some policies
require the bike to be locked to an immovable object.

Weight is the trade-off nobody mentions in the shop. A Diamond-rated U-lock
weighs about 1.5 kilograms. A Silver one weighs half that. The heavy lock is
better and the light lock is the one you will actually carry, and a lock left at
home protects nothing.

That said, the weight problem has an answer. Keep a heavy lock permanently
chained at the place you park most often, and carry a lighter one for everywhere
else. Two locks cost more than one and solve the problem properly. Alternatively,
leave the good bike at home on the days it would sit outside for hours.

## How locks are actually defeated

Bolt croppers account for most thefts of cable and chain locks. A 42-inch pair
goes through anything under 13 millimetres of hardened steel in a few seconds,
and they are easy to carry. Consequently, a cable lock is worth very little.

Angle grinders are the newer problem. A cordless grinder cuts almost any lock,
including Diamond-rated ones, in under a minute. Grinder-resistant locks exist
and are heavy and expensive. Instead, most riders accept the risk and park in
busier places.

However, most thefts are still opportunistic. The bike locked badly next to
yours is the one that goes, which means the marginal value of a better lock is
smaller than the marginal value of locking properly.

In addition, thieves prefer bikes they can identify as valuable. A scruffy bike
with a good lock is a poor target twice over. Indeed, a worn saddle and some
tape on the frame do more than another twenty pounds of lock.

## Locking properly

Get the lock through the frame and through the rear wheel. A lock through the
front wheel alone leaves the thief with the bike.

Fill the U with as much bike and stand as it will take. Empty space inside the
shackle is room for a jack or a lever.

Position the keyhole facing down or against the frame. It makes picking harder
and, more usefully, makes an attack look awkward.

For example, a lock mounted at knee height with the shackle clear of the ground
gives a thief somewhere to brace. The same lock high on the frame does not.

In addition, choose the stand carefully. A signpost that can be lifted out, a
railing with a cut section, a scaffold pole — all defeat any lock at all.

Notably, the cheapest improvement is location. A bike in view of a shop window
is meaningfully safer than one round the corner, and this costs nothing.

## Registration and recovery

Register the frame number with a national scheme before anything happens. It is
free and takes five minutes.

Photograph the bike, including the frame number and any distinguishing marks.
Police recover bikes they cannot return because nobody can prove ownership.

That said, recovery rates are low. Registration is worth doing and is not a
substitute for the lock.

Finally, tell your insurer what you own. An expensive bike is often excluded
from a standard home policy above a threshold, and a specific item listing
usually costs less than people expect.
