# I didn't learn to cook until I was thirty-four

I'm not going to pretend there's a good reason for that. I just didn't, and then
one day I did, and the gap between those two states turned out to be much smaller
than I'd spent fifteen years assuming.

What I'd been telling myself was that cooking was a skill you either picked up at
home or didn't. My mum cooked; I watched telly. By the time I noticed that this
was a choice rather than a fact about me, I'd built up enough embarrassment about
not knowing that asking anyone felt impossible. You can't ask a friend how to
boil pasta when you're thirty and they've eaten at your flat.

So I ate the same nine things for a decade. I'm not exaggerating. Nine.

## What actually changed

A flatmate moved out and took the microwave. That's it. That's the whole story.

I couldn't afford a new one that month, and after about a week of increasingly
grim sandwiches I opened the cupboard properly for the first time and thought,
right, there's a pan in here. How hard can it be.

It was quite hard, at first, in ways that had nothing to do with the food. The
timing was what got me. Everything I read assumed I could have three things
happening at once, and I couldn't — I'd get the onions on, panic about the rice,
come back to find the onions had gone from translucent to charcoal in what felt
like eleven seconds. Nobody tells you that "soften the onions" means standing
there and doing nothing else for eight minutes.

That's the first real thing I learned, and it's not a recipe. It's that most
early cooking failures are scheduling failures.

## Things I got wrong for embarrassingly long

**Heat.** I cooked everything on medium because high felt reckless and low felt
like nothing was happening. It turns out almost everything wants either much
higher heat or much lower heat than medium, and medium is where food goes to be
disappointing. Searing needs a pan hot enough to worry you. A stew wants heat so
low you have to check it's still on.

**Salt.** I was salting at the end, once, like a garnish. You salt in layers,
throughout, and you taste as you go. I'd never tasted anything mid-cooking. I
genuinely didn't know that was allowed.

**Sharpening.** I used the same knife for four years without once sharpening it,
and I'd concluded I was bad at chopping. I wasn't bad at chopping. I was pushing
a blunt piece of metal through an onion.

**Reading the whole recipe first.** Obvious. Took me about a year.

## The thing about recipes

I'd assumed a recipe was a set of instructions, and that following them correctly
would produce the picture. Which is roughly true for baking and roughly false for
everything else.

Most savoury recipes are a description of a shape. Fry an aromatic base, add a
main ingredient, add liquid, cook until done, adjust. Once you've made about
fifteen things you start seeing the shape underneath, and at that point recipes
become suggestions rather than instructions, and cooking stops being an exam.

That shift is the whole thing, really. Before it, every dish is a separate skill
you don't have. After it, most dishes are variations on four or five things you
do have.

I'd say it took me about eight months to get there, cooking maybe four times a
week. Not fifteen years. Eight months.

## What I'd tell someone starting now

Cook the same dish six times before moving on. This is the opposite of what's fun
and it's the fastest way to learn anything. The sixth time, you'll be paying
attention to the food instead of the instructions, and that's when you actually
notice things.

Buy one good knife and learn to sharpen it. Not a set. One.

Don't start with something you'll be judged on. Cooking for a date, in the first
month, is how people decide they can't cook. Cook for yourself on a Tuesday when
it doesn't matter if it's bad.

Accept that you'll waste food. I threw out a lot in that first year and I felt
awful about it, and there wasn't a way round it. You're paying for the lessons
somehow.

And taste everything, constantly. I still think that's the single biggest
difference between people who cook well and people who don't, and it costs
nothing.

## Where I've got to

I'm not good, exactly. I'm competent, which is a much more useful thing to be. I
can look in the fridge and make dinner without a plan. I can adjust something
that's gone wrong instead of starting again. I know what I like and roughly how
to get it.

What surprised me most is how much of it was confidence rather than technique.
The techniques are genuinely simple — there are maybe a dozen that matter, and
you can learn any one of them in an afternoon. What took the time was believing
I was allowed to try, and then getting comfortable with being bad at it in my own
kitchen where nobody could see.

If you're where I was, the thing I'd want you to know is that the fifteen years
weren't about ability. They were about not having started.
