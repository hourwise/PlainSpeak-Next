# Notes on revising a draft

The first draft is not the writing. It is the raw material the writing gets made
from, and treating it as anything more precious than that is the single most
expensive habit a writer can pick up.

I have watched people spend an hour on a sentence in a paragraph they were about
to delete. I have done it myself, more than once, and the reason is always the
same: the sentence was in front of me and the shape of the whole piece was not.
Local work is comfortable. You can see the result immediately. Structural work
means holding the entire thing in your head at once and admitting that a section
you were pleased with has to go.

So the order matters. Structure first, then paragraphs, then sentences, then
words. Anyone who tells you they revise the other way round is describing a
first draft they got lucky with.

## Cutting

Start by asking what the piece is actually about. Not what you meant it to be
about — what it turned out to be about once you had written it. Those are
frequently different things, and the difference is usually where the good
material is hiding. A draft that begins with three paragraphs of throat-clearing
and then says something interesting in the fourth is a draft whose real opening
is the fourth paragraph.

Cutting the first three feels like losing work. It is not. Those paragraphs did
their job: they got you to the fourth one. They were scaffolding, and scaffolding
comes down.

The harder cuts are the ones where the material is good but does not belong. A
digression can be the best thing in a draft and still be wrong for it. Keep it
somewhere else if you like. Do not keep it here on the grounds that it took a
long time.

## Rhythm

Once the shape is right, read it aloud. Not silently while imagining reading it
aloud — actually aloud, at speaking volume, ideally standing up. Your ear will
catch things your eye has stopped seeing, because your eye has read this draft
eleven times and has long since started skipping.

What the ear catches, mostly, is monotony. Sentences that are all the same length
put a listener to sleep in about forty seconds. It does not matter whether they
are long or short. Fourteen consecutive nine-word sentences and fourteen
consecutive thirty-word sentences fail in exactly the same way, which is that
nothing in the sequence tells the listener where the emphasis is.

Variation is how emphasis works in prose. A run of medium-length sentences
followed by a short one puts weight on the short one. That is the whole
mechanism. It is not subtle and it is not decorative — it is the closest thing
written English has to tone of voice, and a draft with no variation has thrown
it away.

Which is why the fix is never "make the sentences shorter". It is "make them
different from each other".

## Openings

Look at how your paragraphs begin. If four of them start with the same
construction, you have a tic, and the reader will notice it before you do. This
is one of those problems that is invisible from the inside and obvious from six
feet away, which is why printing a draft and looking at it from across the desk
is a genuinely useful trick rather than a superstition.

The same applies to sentences within a paragraph. "The system does this. The
system does that. The system also does the other." Three sentences, one shape,
and the reader has stopped listening by the third.

I would not go so far as to say a repeated opening is always wrong. Repetition
is sometimes exactly the effect you want — deliberate anaphora is one of the
oldest tools in the box, and it works. The difference is whether you chose it. A
writer who repeats an opening four times for rhythm has made a decision. A writer
who does it because it was the easiest way to start each paragraph has not.

## Signposting

Transitions are load-bearing when a reader might genuinely lose the thread, and
padding when they would not. "However" earns its place when the next sentence
reverses the last one. It earns nothing at all when the next sentence simply
continues.

The test I use is to delete the transition and read the two sentences together.
If the relationship between them is still clear, the transition was doing
nothing, and prose with a great deal of nothing in it reads as heavy without
being able to say why.

There is a failure mode in the other direction too. Strip out every connective
and you get a sequence of assertions with no visible relationship, which is
harder to read, not easier. The goal is not fewer transitions. It is transitions
that are carrying something.

## Words

Last, and least. Word-level revision is the part everyone enjoys and the part
that matters least, which is not an argument for skipping it — it is an argument
for doing it last, when the structure underneath is settled enough to be worth
polishing.

Two things to watch. The first is the word you have fallen in love with this
month. Everyone has one. Mine changes about twice a year and I only ever notice
it when someone points out that I have used it six times on one page. Search for
your own; you probably know what it is.

The second is the phrase that announces rather than states. "It is important to
note that the deadline is Friday" is a longer way of saying the deadline is
Friday, and the extra words do not add emphasis. They dilute it. The reader
already knows you think it is important, because you wrote it down.

## When to stop

You stop when the changes you are making are lateral — when you are swapping one
adequate word for another adequate word and could swap it back tomorrow without
noticing. That is the signal. It arrives later than you would like and earlier
than perfectionism will let you believe.

Give it to someone else at that point. Not for approval. For the specific
question of where they got lost, which is the one thing you can no longer
determine on your own, having lost the ability to read this piece for the first
time somewhere around draft two.
