# What to carry, and what to leave

Every list of kit you will read online is written by someone with different legs,
a different tolerance for cold and a different idea of what counts as an
emergency. Mine is no exception. What follows is what I actually carry on a long
day in British hills between about March and October, and — more usefully — why.

The general principle is that weight you carry all day costs you something on
every step, and weight you might need once costs you nothing on the day you need
it. Almost every argument about kit is really an argument about where to draw
that line, and reasonable people draw it in different places.

## The things I never leave behind

These four are not negotiable, and I would turn back for any of them:

- **A map and compass, and the ability to use them.** The map is the cheap part.
  Being able to take a bearing in cloud, on ground you cannot see, is the part
  that takes practice, and it is not a skill that survives being learned once.
- **Waterproofs, top and bottom.** Not a shower jacket. Something that will keep
  working after three hours of horizontal rain, because that is the condition
  they exist for.
- **More warm layers than the forecast suggests.** The summit is colder than the
  car park by roughly a degree for every 150 metres of ascent, and that is before
  wind.
- **Food you will actually eat when you are cold and miserable.** This is a
  surprisingly personal question. Whatever it is, it is not the thing you bought
  because the packaging said it was efficient.

None of that is controversial. The interesting decisions start after it.

## Things I carry that other people think are unnecessary

A head torch, in June, on a day walk that ends at four. I have twice been very
glad of this and never once regretted the ninety grams. A twisted ankle at three
in the afternoon turns a four-hour day into an eight-hour one, and the second
half of it is in the dark.

A paper map even when the phone is working. Phones die in cold weather with no
warning at all — a battery reading forty per cent at the top can be nothing by
the time you want it — and a map does not care about temperature.

Two litres of water on a hot day, which is heavy and which I resent for the first
hour. Streams in the hills are not reliably drinkable and are not reliably
present.

## Things I have stopped carrying

Here I have gone the other way over the years, mostly by noticing that certain
items came home unused for years at a stretch:

- A full first aid kit. I now carry tape, a couple of dressings, painkillers and
  a blister kit, and nothing else. The rest was reassurance rather than equipment.
- A stove. Lovely on a summer col; forty minutes I did not have on most days.
- Spare boots laces, a multi-tool, and a second hat.

I want to be careful about how that reads. The point is not that these things are
useless. It is that I had been carrying them for eight years without needing
them, on terrain and in seasons where the consequence of not having them was
inconvenience rather than danger. Change the terrain and the answer changes.

## The bag itself

Thirty litres is about right for a long day. Twenty is achievable and means
skipping something. Forty means you will fill it, because everyone fills whatever
they bring.

Two things matter more than capacity. The first is whether it carries weight on
your hips rather than your shoulders, which is a question of fit and cannot be
answered from a photograph. The second is whether you can reach your waterproofs
without taking the whole thing off, because a jacket that requires a full unpack
in driving rain is a jacket you will put on ten minutes too late.

## Weather, which is the actual subject

All of this is really a conversation about weather, and the honest summary is
that the British hills produce winter conditions in months that are not winter.
Snow on the Cairngorms in June is not remarkable. Wind chill on a Lakeland ridge
in September can take the effective temperature below freezing on a day that was
pleasant in the valley.

The forecast to read is a mountain forecast, not a town one, and the number to
look at is not the temperature. It is the wind speed, the freezing level and the
cloud base — in that order. A still day at four degrees is fine. Four degrees
with a forty-mile-an-hour wind is a different day entirely, and the ridge you
planned is now a decision rather than a route.

## Turning round

The last piece of kit is the willingness to not finish.

I know that sounds like the sort of thing people say. It is also the only item on
this page that has actually kept me out of trouble, twice. Both times the
conditions were worse than forecast, both times I was two thirds of the way up,
and both times the thing that made it hard was not the weather. It was having
told people where I was going.

Decide your turnaround time before you set off, and treat it as a rule rather
than a suggestion. The hill will be there in a fortnight. Set a point on the map
as well as a time, because on a long slog the clock and the ground disagree, and
whichever you hit first is the one that counts.
