# A year on plot 14

I took the plot on in February, which everyone told me was the wrong month and
which turned out to be exactly right, because February is when you can see what
you have actually got.

Nothing was growing. The couch grass had died back to a mat of pale roots just
under the surface, the brambles along the north fence were bare enough to show
where they came from, and the previous tenant's raised beds — four of them,
railway sleepers, rotted at the corners — were visible as beds rather than as
green mounds. In July I would have inherited a jungle and spent three weeks
finding out what was underneath it. In February I could stand at the gate and see
the whole thing.

So: February. Take the plot in February.

## What the first month was actually like

Cold, mostly. I had imagined something more agricultural and it was closer to
demolition. Four days of pulling brambles, which is not gardening, and then two
weeks of lifting couch grass root by root, which is not gardening either but is
at least quiet.

Couch grass is the thing nobody warns you about properly. Every fragment of root
you leave behind becomes a new plant. You cannot rotavate it — that just chops
the roots into hundreds of pieces and multiplies your problem. You cannot really
smother it in one season either, though people will tell you otherwise. What you
can do is lift it by hand, slowly, working a fork under the mat and shaking the
soil off, and accept that you will be doing it again in autumn and probably the
autumn after that.

I found a shoe. I found nine feet of blue polypropylene rope, a milk bottle from
a dairy that closed in 1998, and roughly forty metres of weed-suppressant
membrane that had been laid down and then covered with soil, which is the
gardening equivalent of putting a plaster over a splinter.

## Getting things in the ground

The first sowing went in at the end of March and about half of it came to
nothing, which I am told is normal and which felt at the time like a verdict.

Broad beans were fine. Broad beans are always fine; they are the plant equivalent
of a friend who says yes to everything. Parsnips did not germinate at all, and I
have since learned that parsnip seed loses viability in a single year and mine
was three years old and had been in a shed. Radishes came up in nine days and
were the first thing I ate off the plot, which mattered more than the radishes
warranted.

The potatoes were the surprise. I planted two rows of Charlotte because they were
on offer, expected very little, and got about eleven kilos. They also did the
useful structural work of breaking up the ground where the couch grass had been
worst — not because potatoes magically clear weeds, whatever you read, but
because earthing them up twice means you disturb that ground twice at exactly the
moment the weeds are trying to re-establish.

## Midsummer

By June the plot looked like an allotment rather than a building site, and I
started making the mistake everyone makes, which is planting more than I could
water.

There is a particular kind of optimism that hits in May. The soil is warm, the
seedlings are all alive, and there is space. So you fill the space. Then July
arrives, it does not rain for three weeks, you are carrying eleven watering cans
up from the tap every evening after work, and you begin to understand why the
older plotholders leave a third of their ground under green manure.

I lost most of the salad. The lettuces bolted in four days during the hot spell
and went bitter, the rocket went to seed almost immediately, and the chard — which
I had been slightly sniffy about — carried on entirely unbothered and fed us
until November.

Chard is the answer. Nobody wants to hear this. Chard is the answer.

## Things I got wrong

The paths. I laid them out in spring at a width that made sense when the beds
were empty, and by August I could not get a wheelbarrow between two rows of
climbing beans. Paths need to be wider than looks sensible. Everything grows
sideways as well as up.

The compost. I built a bay out of pallets, filled it with everything, and did not
turn it once. What I have now is a wet anaerobic mass that smells wrong and will
take another year. The neighbouring plot turns theirs monthly and had usable
compost by September. This is not a subtle technique I failed to master; it is
simply a job I did not do.

The netting. I put brassicas in without it, on the theory that I would keep an
eye on them. Cabbage white butterflies do not care about your intentions. Nine
plants, stripped to the ribs, in under a fortnight.

## What I would tell someone starting

Take less ground than you are offered, at least for the first year. A half plot
you keep on top of teaches you far more than a full plot that defeats you in
June, and there is no prize for having the biggest patch of weeds on the site.

Talk to the people who have been there twenty years. They will tell you things
about your specific soil, your specific slope and your specific wind that no book
can, and most of them are visibly delighted to be asked.

Keep a notebook. Not a nice one — a cheap one that can live in the shed and get
rained on. What went in, when, and what happened. By the second year that
notebook is worth more than any of the equipment.

And expect the first season to be about the ground rather than about the
vegetables. You are not really growing food yet. You are finding out what you
have, and putting it into a condition where growing food next year is possible.
The eleven kilos of potatoes were nice. The two weeks of pulling couch grass out
by hand were the actual work.
