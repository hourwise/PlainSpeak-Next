# Ordinary three-part lists

A control document. Three-item lists are among the most natural constructions in
English, and this page uses them the way anyone writing carefully would: often
enough to be visible, not so often that the pattern becomes the point.

Phase 7 could not test this, because no calibration document exercised triadic
repetition from the quiet side at all. The nearest thing the corpus had was a
document measuring 0.29 against a line at 0.30, which is not a margin.

## Cooking

Most stocks begin the same way. You need onion, carrot and celery, cut roughly
and softened in fat before anything else goes in the pan.

The proportions matter less than people think. What matters is that the
vegetables sweat rather than colour, that the liquid never comes above a bare
tremble, and that you skim whatever rises in the first twenty minutes.

A stock made from roasted bones is darker and heavier. A stock made from raw
bones is paler and cleaner. Neither is better; they belong to different dishes.

Cool it quickly, strain it through something finer than a colander, and let it
set overnight. Fat lifts off a cold stock in a sheet.

## Buildings

The three questions to ask about an old building are what it is made of, what is
holding it up, and where the water goes.

The first is usually visible. The second is often not, particularly in buildings
that have been altered, and an internal wall that looks like a partition may be
carrying a floor above it.

Water is the one that causes the damage. A blocked gutter will do more harm over
five winters than a decade of ordinary weather, because the wall never dries and
the mortar goes soft from behind.

Surveyors look at roofs first for exactly this reason.

## Sound

A room does three things to a sound: it reflects some of it, absorbs some of it,
and lets some of it through the walls.

Hard parallel surfaces reflect. Soft irregular ones absorb. Mass — genuine
weight, not thickness — is what stops sound passing through, which is why a heavy
curtain quiets a room without making it any more private.

The common mistake is treating all three as one problem. Foam on the walls of a
noisy office changes how the room sounds to the people in it and does almost
nothing about the conversation the room next door can hear.

## Learning an instrument

Progress on an instrument comes from three things in roughly equal measure: time
at it, attention while you are at it, and a teacher who can hear what you cannot.

The second is the one people skip. Forty minutes of playing a passage through is
worth less than fifteen spent on the two bars that keep going wrong, and it is
considerably less uncomfortable, which is why it happens instead.

Recording yourself is a cheap substitute for the third. Not a good substitute — a
teacher tells you what to do about the problem — but recordings will at least
tell you the problem exists.

## Maps

A map is a set of decisions about what to leave out.

The cartographer chooses a scale, a projection and a set of features, and every
one of those choices removes something. A road atlas that showed every footpath
would be unreadable at motorway speed. An Ordnance Survey sheet that omitted
field boundaries would be useless to a walker.

This is why asking whether a map is accurate is usually the wrong question. The
question is whether it is accurate about the things it was made to show.
