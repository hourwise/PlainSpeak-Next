# Ordinary use of ordinary constructions

Another control. Every construction the rhetorical diagnostic recognises is
perfectly good English, and a writer who never used one would be writing worse
prose, not better. This page uses several of them the way they normally get used:
scattered, for the job each does, rather than as a habit.

## Tides

Whether the tide is rising or falling matters more than how high it is, at least
if you are trying to get somewhere.

A flooding tide in a narrow channel can run at four knots, which is faster than
most people can row and faster than a fair number of small engines can push
against. The height tells you whether the water is deep enough. The direction and
the rate tell you whether you are going anywhere.

Tide tables give you both, though the second takes a little arithmetic. The rule
of twelfths is a rough guide: a twelfth of the range in the first hour, two in
the second, three in each of the middle two, then two and one. It is not exact
and it is close enough to plan by.

## Bicycle gears

The number of gears on a bicycle is not just a marketing figure, though it is
often treated as one.

What matters is the range — the ratio between the easiest gear and the hardest —
and the size of the steps between them. A bike with eleven closely spaced gears
and a bike with eight widely spaced ones can have the same range, and they suit
different riding. Close spacing lets you hold a cadence on rolling ground. Wide
spacing gets you up a hill you would otherwise walk.

Both matter, and the one you should care about depends entirely on where you
ride.

## Bread

It is not the flour that decides whether a loaf works, but the handling.

Strong flour helps. A long fermentation helps more. What actually determines the
crumb is how much gas the dough can hold and for how long, and that comes from
gluten development and from not knocking all the air out at the shaping stage.

Bakers argue about this endlessly. The argument is usually about which variable
is worth the most attention, not about whether the others exist.

## Editing photographs

From the raw file to the finished print, almost every step is a choice, and most
of them are invisible in the result.

White balance, exposure, contrast and crop are decisions a photographer makes
before anyone else sees the picture. That is not manipulation in any interesting
sense; it is the same set of decisions that was previously made in a darkroom
with dodging, burning and paper grade.

The line people usually want to draw is between changing how a scene looks and
changing what was in it, and that line is clearer in principle than it is at the
edges.

## Reading music

Sight-reading is more than simply recognising the notes.

It is pattern recognition under time pressure: seeing a scale fragment as a
scale, an arpeggio as an arpeggio, a familiar cadence as a shape rather than four
separate pitches. Players who read well are not reading faster. They are reading
larger units.

Which is why the way to get better at it is to read a great deal of easy music
rather than a little difficult music, and why almost nobody does that.

## Insulation

Whether insulation pays for itself depends on the house, the fuel and how long
you intend to stay.

Loft insulation is nearly always worth it: cheap, quick, and the heat that
escapes upwards is the heat you lose most of. Solid wall insulation is a
different calculation entirely, running from several thousand pounds to rather
more, and the payback period can exceed the time most people own a house.

Neither figure is the whole story. A cold house is uncomfortable as well as
expensive, and comfort does not appear in the arithmetic at all.
