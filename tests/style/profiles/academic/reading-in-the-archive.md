# Reading practices and the shape of the archive

What survives in an archive is not a sample of what once existed, and the ways in
which it is not a sample are systematic rather than random. This is a familiar
observation, but its methodological consequences are frequently acknowledged in a
preface and then set aside for the remainder of an argument.

The difficulty is that the selection pressures which determine survival tend to
correlate with the variables historians are interested in. Institutional records
survive better than domestic ones; printed matter survives better than
manuscript; documents held by wealthy households survive better than those held
by poor ones. A study of reading practices that draws on surviving evidence is
therefore studying, in the first instance, the reading practices of people whose
possessions were durable, valued and stored — which is a description of a
population, not a neutral window onto a period.

## Inventories and their silences

Probate inventories illustrate the problem with unusual clarity, since they
appear at first to offer exactly what is wanted: a systematic, dated, itemised
record of what people owned.

Yet the appearance of systematicity is misleading in at least three respects.
Inventories were compiled for a legal purpose, and that purpose determined what
was worth recording; items below a threshold of value were routinely omitted or
bundled into an undifferentiated category. Cheap print — the ballads, chapbooks
and almanacs that constituted the bulk of what most people read — falls almost
entirely below that threshold. An inventory that lists "bookes in the parlour,
xij d." has told us that books were present and almost nothing about which ones.

Furthermore, inventories were made at death, which means they describe holdings
at the end of a life rather than across it. A reader who owned and disposed of
material throughout adulthood appears in the record only as the residue. Where
the disposal was itself patterned — where devotional works were kept and
entertainment was not, for example — the residue is not merely smaller than the
lifetime holding but different in kind.

Finally, and perhaps most importantly, inventories were made for households
above a certain wealth. The population they describe is not the reading
population.

## Marginalia and the sample it defines

Marginalia present the opposite difficulty. Where inventories are systematic but
silent about content, annotations are richly specific and wholly unsystematic.

An annotated copy tells us a great deal about one reader's engagement with one
text: which passages were attended to, what was contested, how the reading was
organised. This is precisely the evidence that inventories cannot supply. The
problem is that annotation is a minority practice, that annotated copies survive
preferentially because they were valued as objects, and that the surviving
annotations skew heavily towards readers with the training and the confidence to
write in books.

It does not follow that marginalia are uninformative. It follows that
generalisation from them requires an argument about the relation between the
annotating minority and the reading population, and such arguments are more often
gestured at than made.

## Combining evidence

A common response is to triangulate: to use inventories for extent, marginalia
for depth, and booksellers' records for circulation, on the assumption that the
biases of each will be partially independent.

The assumption deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives. The three sources
are not independently biased; they are biased in substantially the same
direction, because each preferentially records the activity of the same wealthy,
literate, urban minority. Combining three sources that over-represent the same
population produces a better-evidenced account of that population rather than a
correction towards the whole.

Triangulation is nonetheless worth doing, provided the claim made for it is the
right one. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement locates a genuine
question. Where they agree, the agreement is weaker evidence than it appears,
because shared bias produces shared answers.

## Reading against the record

The more productive strategy, in my view, is to read the record for the shape of
its silences rather than around them.

An inventory that bundles cheap print into a single line is evidence that cheap
print was present in quantities not worth itemising, which is itself a claim
about circulation. A bookseller's ledger that records credit relationships tells
us about the social organisation of purchase even where the titles are illegible.
The absence of annotation in a heavily worn copy is evidence of a reading
practice, not merely of missing data.

This approach has its own risks. Arguments from silence are notoriously
accommodating, and a sufficiently ingenious reader can extract confirmation of
almost any hypothesis from an absence. The discipline required is to state in
advance what the silence would look like under the alternative hypothesis, and to
check whether the record distinguishes them. Frequently it does not, and saying
so is more useful than an ingenious reading.

## Implications

Three implications seem to me to follow.

Studies of reading should specify the population their evidence can support
claims about, and should resist the temptation to let that population expand
silently between the methods section and the conclusion. This is a matter of
stating what is already known rather than of discovering anything new.

Quantitative work on inventories should report the recording threshold and its
implications for the categories being counted, since a count of books is a count
of books above a value, and the two are not interchangeable.

And archival absence should be treated as data with its own structure rather than
as a regrettable gap in coverage. The archive is not a damaged copy of the past.
It is the record of what particular institutions, for particular reasons, thought
worth keeping — and that is a subject in itself, not only an obstacle to another
one.

## A note on scale

Digitisation has changed what is tractable without changing what survives, and it
is worth being explicit about which of those two facts a given argument depends
on.

Large-scale text collections permit questions that manuscript work could not
address: frequency across decades, the diffusion of a phrase, the co-occurrence
of terms in thousands of volumes. What they do not do is enlarge the archive.
A digitised collection reproduces the selection pressures of the physical one
that was scanned, and typically adds a further layer — the priorities of the
institution that funded the scanning — on top.

The consequence is that a corpus study can be simultaneously more powerful and
less representative than the manuscript work it supersedes, and the second half
of that sentence tends to receive less attention than the first.
